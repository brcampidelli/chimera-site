The AI industry is grappling with a fundamental tension: the push for open models versus the reality of hardware-driven centralization. This debate isn’t just theoretical—it has practical implications for anyone building AI agents. While open-source frameworks promise democratization, the underlying infrastructure often consolidates power in the hands of a few. This dynamic forces agent builders to navigate a landscape where access to computational resources can be as critical as the models themselves. 

## The Hardware Bottleneck

The recent valuation surge of Etched, a company specializing in AI hardware, underscores the growing importance of computational infrastructure [[2]](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/18/etcheds-valuation-doubles-to-21b-in-a-month/). Their systems, now deployed by major players like Jane Street, highlight how hardware can become a bottleneck for innovation. Even with open models, the ability to run them efficiently often depends on proprietary hardware or cloud services controlled by a handful of companies. This creates a paradox: while open models theoretically level the playing field, the cost and complexity of deploying them at scale can reinforce existing power structures.

## The Open Model Illusion

Anthropic’s CEO Dario Amodei argues that open models alone don’t solve the centralization problem—they merely shift power to those who control the chips [[3]](https://the-decoder.com/anthropic-ceo-says-ai-centralizes-by-nature-and-open-models-just-shift-power-to-whoever-owns-the-chips/). This perspective challenges the notion that open-source AI is inherently democratic. For agent builders, this means that relying solely on open models without considering the infrastructure required to deploy them can lead to unintended dependencies. The debate also raises questions about regulation: if hardware centralization is inevitable, how can policymakers ensure fair access and competition?

## Practical Implications for Agent Builders

For those building AI agents, this debate highlights the need to think beyond model selection. While open-source frameworks like Chimera Agent offer flexibility, their effectiveness often depends on the underlying infrastructure. Agent builders must consider not just the models they use, but also the computational resources required to deploy them. This might involve exploring partnerships with hardware providers, optimizing for resource efficiency, or advocating for policies that promote fair access to computational power. Ultimately, the centralization debate isn’t just about who controls AI—it’s about how builders can navigate this complex landscape to create agents that are both powerful and accessible.
